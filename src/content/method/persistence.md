You found the right account, the right person and the right proof. You sent a short, specific email with a small ask. And nothing came back.

That's normal. Good first emails get read, appreciated and then buried by a busy week. The fourth part of the Repeat Success Method is about what you do next.

## Don't send "just checking in"

The worst follow-up is the one that asks for attention without giving anything back. "Just checking in." "Bumping this to the top of your inbox." "Did you see my last email?" Each one tells the buyer you have nothing new to say.

> Don't send "just checking in." Give them something new.

Every follow-up should add value on its own. If someone read only that one message, it should still be worth their time.

## What to send instead

Here are new things you can bring to each touch:

- **A one-page before and after.** Show how a similar customer worked before and after the change.
- **A short video.** Walk through one idea in a minute or two.
- **An article about their industry.** Something relevant to the reason you wrote in the first place.
- **Another customer's result.** A second proof point, ideally from the same industry.
- **News from their company.** A new announcement, a new hire or something their leadership said publicly.

Here is what a follow-up can look like, using the same made-up example from the first email:

> Hi Dana, one more thing on the warehouse. Here is a one-page look at how the freight company I mentioned planned its routes before and after. If it's useful, I'm happy to walk through it in 15 to 20 minutes.

It ties back to the reason you wrote. It gives her something useful. And the ask stays small.

## Use more than one channel

Email is only one way in. A real outreach cadence mixes channels: email, phone and, where it fits, in person. A short call after your first email gives your name a voice. A follow-up email after a voicemail gives the voicemail something to point to.

A word on texting: be careful. A phone number from a data tool is not consent to text someone. Texting carries the most risk of any channel, so don't treat it as a shortcut. Know your company's rules and the law where your buyer is before you send a text.

## Keep the ask the same size

As the touches add up, it's tempting to ask for more or to push harder. Don't. The ask stays the same: 15 to 20 minutes, to see if you can repeat a similar customer's success for them. An invitation, not a pitch.

What changes is what you bring. What stays the same is respect for their time.

## Before you send: five checks

Run every first email and every follow-up through these five checks:

1. I have one real, specific reason for writing to this person.
2. My customer story has a challenge and a number I'm approved to use.
3. The ask is 15 to 20 minutes, and nothing bigger.
4. It fits on a phone screen without scrolling.
5. I checked every fact AI gave me and rewrote it in my own voice.

If any check fails, fix it before you hit send.

## One thing to do today

Look at the accounts you emailed in the last two weeks with no reply. For each one, find one new thing to send: an article, a second customer result or a piece of their own news. That's your follow-up.

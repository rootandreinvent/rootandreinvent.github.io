Once you know which accounts to call first, the next question is who to call. This is the second part of the Repeat Success Method, and it's where a lot of good outreach quietly fails. The email was researched. The proof was strong. It just went to someone who couldn't say yes, or who didn't care about the problem.

## Follow the money

Here is the rule I come back to:

> Sales and finance usually own the money, so know who you're selling to and what they care about.

That doesn't mean every email goes to the CFO. It means you should always know where the budget sits and how your conversation will eventually get there. Before you write, answer two questions:

1. **Who feels the problem?** The person living with it every day.
2. **Who pays to fix it?** The person whose budget it comes out of.

Sometimes that is the same person. Often it isn't. Knowing the difference shapes everything about your first email: the reason you give, the proof you pick and the size of the ask.

## Start close to the pain and earn your way up

In most accounts, start with the people closest to the pain and earn your way up.

The person closest to the pain knows the problem in detail. They can tell you what has been tried, what failed and who else cares. They are also the person most likely to take a 15 to 20 minute meeting about it, because the problem is theirs.

From there you earn the next conversation. A useful first meeting gives you what you need to go higher: the real challenge in their words, the cost of leaving it alone, and the names of the people who sign off. When you do reach the person who owns the money, you aren't a cold email anymore. You're someone their team already found worth talking to.

## The exception: when to go straight to the top

There are times to skip the climb. Two situations are often enough to ask the CEO directly for a 15 to 20 minute call:

- **A smaller company where you have a real point of view.** In a smaller company the CEO is often close to the problem anyway. If you have a specific, well-researched view on their business, it can be worth their time.
- **An account with several dead opportunities and notes from the previous rep.** If the company has looked at this problem more than once and stalled each time, the person at the top may want to know why. The history gives you a reason to write.

The bar for this is higher, not lower. A note to a CEO needs the most specific reason, the strongest proof and the smallest ask.

## Know what they care about

Finding the right name is half the work. The other half is knowing what that person is measured on. A sales leader cares about pipeline and the number. A finance leader cares about cost, risk and return. Someone running operations cares about the work getting done with the people they have.

The same customer story can land very differently depending on how you frame the result. Lead with the part of the outcome that matters to the person reading it.

## Make your subject line about them

Once you have the right person, the subject line is your first chance to show it was written for them. Short and specific wins. A few patterns that work:

- **Their news:** "Your second warehouse"
- **The outcome:** "Fewer missed deliveries after expansion"
- **A question:** "Question about the new product launch"
- **Their words:** quote something they said publicly about a priority
- **A connection:** "[Mutual connection] suggested I reach out"

Avoid your company name, all caps, and "quick question" with nothing behind it. *(These examples are made up.)*

## One thing to do today

Take your Tier 1 accounts. For each one, write down two names: the person closest to the pain and the person who owns the money. If you can't name both, that's your research for this week.
